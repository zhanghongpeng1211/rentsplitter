I Lived With a Couple for a Year. Here's the Real Math on What They Should Pay.

By Jake Morrison 2026-07-26 5 min read

I've written about the couple problem before, but I've never lived with a couple for a full year. Until Apartment 8. Alex and Jordan moved in as a couple, sharing one bedroom, and I was the third roommate in the other bedroom. Three people. Two rooms. One year. Here's what the math actually looked like.

Rent was $2,250. The rooms were identical: 160 sq ft each. Same closet. Same window. Same everything. By square footage, we should have paid $750 each. But there were three people, not two. And two of them shared a room.

We tried three different splits over the year:

**Months 1-3: Equal thirds.** $750 each. This was simple and seemed fair. But Alex and Jordan were using the kitchen twice as much, running the dishwasher daily, and taking twice as many showers. I was subsidizing their lifestyle.

**Months 4-6: Couple pays 1.5x.** Alex and Jordan paid $1,125 total ($562.50 each), I paid $750. This was better for me but Alex felt like they were being punished for being in a relationship. The kitchen usage was still uneven but at least the rent reflected the people count.

**Months 7-12: Hybrid system.** Rent split by room: $750 per room. Then utilities split per person. So rent was $750 for me, $1,125 for the couple. But utilities ($310/month) were split three ways: $103 each. This was the system that stuck because it separated "room cost" from "people cost."

Here's the annual breakdown:

**Equal thirds:** I paid $9,000 total ($750 rent + $103 utilities x 12). Couple paid $18,000 total.

**1.5x split:** I paid $9,000 total. Couple paid $19,500 total.

**Hybrid:** I paid $9,000 total. Couple paid $19,500 total. (Same as 1.5x for the couple, but the logic was clearer.)

The difference for me was $0 across all three methods because my room was the same size. The difference for the couple was $1,500 a year. That's real money, but it's also fair. Two people use more resources than one. That's not an opinion. That's physics.

The key to making it work wasn't the math. It was the conversation. We sat down before anyone moved in, ran the numbers together, and agreed on a system. When the numbers are agreed upon upfront, nobody feels cheated later.

If you're about to move in with a couple, or you're a couple about to move in with a single person, use the calculator on this site. Set the "people in room" setting to 2. Show the numbers. Let the math be the "bad guy." And if the couple refuses to pay more than a single person, that tells you something about how the next year is going to go.
