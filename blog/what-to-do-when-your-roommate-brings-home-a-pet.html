What to Do When Your Roommate Brings Home a Pet (Without Losing Your Mind)

By Jake Morrison 2026-06-22 5 min read

I came home from work on a Tuesday and there was a dog in my living room. Not a small dog. A medium-sized dog with energy and opinions. My roommate Sarah was sitting on the floor with it, looking guilty and excited at the same time.

"His name is Max," she said. "I got him from the shelter today. I know I should have asked first but he was going to be put down and I couldn't —"

I held up my hand. "Stop. Let me process the fact that there is now a dog in our apartment."

I love dogs. I do. But I also love not having dog hair on my couch and not hearing barking at 6am and not paying for vet bills that aren't mine. And our lease specifically said "no pets without written consent from all roommates and the landlord."

We had a conversation that night. Not an argument — a conversation. Because Sarah had made an emotional decision and I needed to make a practical one. Here's what we agreed on:

**Landlord approval:** Sarah had to get written permission from the landlord within 48 hours. If the landlord said no, Max had to go to a backup home Sarah had already identified. (She hadn't. I made her find one before we continued the conversation.)

**Financial responsibility:** Sarah paid a $200 "pet deposit" to the roommate fund. This covered potential damage. She also agreed to pay for all vet bills, food, and supplies. Max was her financial responsibility, not ours.

**Space rules:** Max was not allowed in my room. Ever. He was allowed in common areas but Sarah had to vacuum twice a week. If I found dog hair on my bathroom towel, we had a problem.

**Noise:** If Max barked before 8am or after 10pm, Sarah had to address it immediately. No "he's just adjusting." Immediate.

**Backup plan:** If Sarah moved out, Max moved out. He was not staying with the remaining roommates. This was non-negotiable.

Sarah got landlord approval. Max stayed. He was actually a great dog. He and Biscuit reached a tense but functional truce where they ignored each other from opposite sides of the apartment. And Sarah kept up her end of the deal — she vacuumed, she paid, she managed the noise.

But the lesson is this: pets in shared housing need rules before they need love. The emotional decision has already been made by the time you find out about the pet. Your job is to protect yourself financially and practically. Get it in writing. Set boundaries. And if your roommate won't agree to basic rules, the pet is a symptom of a bigger problem.
