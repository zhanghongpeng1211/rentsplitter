I Tried Splitting Rent by Income for 6 Months. Here's What Actually Happened.

By Jake Morrison 2026-03-22 5 min read

I've always been a square-footage guy when it comes to rent. Room size = rent share. It's objective. It's measurable. It removes emotion from the conversation. But last year, I lived with two roommates who made very different amounts of money, and they suggested we try income-based splitting. I was skeptical but curious. So we tried it for six months.

Here's the setup: three-bedroom apartment, $3,000 total rent. Me: $65,000/year. Sarah: $48,000/year. Tom: $82,000/year. By square footage, we would have paid roughly $1,000, $950, and $1,050. By income, the calculator said we should pay $1,040, $768, and $1,192.

Sarah loved it immediately. She saved $182 a month. That's $2,184 a year. For someone making $48,000, that's real money. Tom was fine with it. He paid $142 more, which was about 2% of his income. I paid $40 more, which was basically a rounding error.

Month one was fine. Month two was fine. By month three, I started noticing something weird: Tom was using the common space way more than anyone else. He worked from home four days a week. He had a standing desk in the living room. He was basically getting a home office for an extra $142 a month while Sarah and I were at our offices.

That's when I realized the problem with income-based splitting: it accounts for money but not usage. Tom made more, so he paid more. But he also used more electricity, more internet, and more space. Sarah made less, so she paid less. But she was barely home. The math was "fair" on paper but not fair in practice.

We adjusted at month four. We kept the income-based rent split but added a usage-based utility adjustment. Tom paid an extra $30/month for being home all day. Sarah got a $15 discount for being home the least. I stayed neutral. The system worked better after that, but it was more complicated than any of us wanted.

By month six, we went back to square footage for rent and per-person for utilities. It was simpler. Everyone understood it. And honestly, the $40 difference between income-based and square-footage for me wasn't worth the monthly negotiation about who used what.

Here's my conclusion: income-based rent splitting works best when everyone has similar usage patterns and very different incomes. If one person makes $30,000 and another makes $90,000, income splitting prevents the lower earner from being rent-burdened. But if usage patterns are very different, you need a hybrid system. And hybrid systems are complicated.

If you're considering income-based splitting, run both methods through the calculator on this site. Compare the numbers. Talk about usage. And be honest about whether you're willing to have a monthly conversation about who used more electricity. If the answer is no, stick with square footage. It's not perfect, but it's simple. And simple is underrated.
