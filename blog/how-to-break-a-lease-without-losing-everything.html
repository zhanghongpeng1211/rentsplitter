How to Break a Lease Without Losing Everything (I've Done It Twice)

By Jake Morrison 2026-05-30 5 min read

I've broken two leases in 8 years. Not because I'm irresponsible — okay, maybe a little irresponsible — but because life happens. Job changes. Relationship changes. The realization that your roommate is actually a cult recruiter. (That last one was a joke. Mostly.)

The first time, I lost $800. The second time, I lost $0. The difference was that the second time, I knew what I was doing. Here's what I learned.

**Step 1: Read your lease.** I know. Reading leases is boring. But the lease will tell you exactly what happens if you break it. Some leases have a "break clause" that lets you leave early for a fee. Some require 30 or 60 days notice. Some say you're on the hook for the full remaining rent. Know what you signed before you do anything.

**Step 2: Talk to your landlord immediately.** Don't wait until you're desperate. The earlier you communicate, the more options you have. Most landlords would rather work with you than evict you. Eviction is expensive and time-consuming for them too.

**Step 3: Find a replacement tenant.** This is the most important step. If you can find someone to take over your lease, most landlords will let you out with minimal or no penalty. I found my replacement for Apartment 4 through a Facebook housing group. It took two weeks. The landlord met them, approved them, and I walked away with my full deposit.

**Step 4: Document the apartment condition.** Take photos. Lots of photos. If the landlord tries to keep your deposit for "damages," you want proof that the apartment is in the same condition it was when you moved in. This is especially important if you're leaving early and the landlord is already annoyed.

**Step 5: Get everything in writing.** If the landlord agrees to let you out of the lease, get it in writing. An email is fine. "Per our conversation, I am being released from my lease effective [date] with no penalty, and my security deposit of $[amount] will be returned within 30 days." If they won't put it in writing, that's a red flag.

The first time I broke a lease, I did none of these things. I panicked, gave 10 days notice, didn't find a replacement, and the landlord kept my $800 deposit plus charged me an extra month's rent. Total cost: $1,550. For a $950 apartment. I was 24 and didn't know better.

The second time, I followed all five steps. I gave 60 days notice. I found a replacement in three weeks. I documented everything. I got a written agreement. I got my full $1,200 deposit back. Total cost: $0.

Breaking a lease is not the end of the world. It's a business transaction. Treat it like one. Communicate early, find a replacement, document everything, and get it in writing. The difference between losing $1,550 and losing $0 is just planning.
