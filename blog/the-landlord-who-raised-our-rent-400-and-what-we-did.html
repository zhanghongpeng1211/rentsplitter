The Landlord Who Raised Our Rent 400 And What We Did

By Jake Morrison 2026-07-27 6 min read min read

$400. That was the number staring back at me from my phone screen at 7:42 on a Tuesday morning. Not a typo. Not a prank. Just a single line in an email from our landlord saying that starting next month, our rent would jump from $1,800 to $2,200. I literally stared at it for five minutes, waiting for my brain to process what I was reading.

My hands were shaking a little. Not from fear, exactly. More from that specific kind of anger that starts in your chest and spreads outward until your fingertips feel hot. I had been living in this apartment for three years. Never late on rent. Never caused problems. And this was the thanks we got?

I walked into the kitchen — well, stomped, if I'm being honest — and found my roommate Marcus already there, coffee in hand, looking like someone had just run over his dog. "You got the email too," he said. It wasn't a question.

Our third roommate, Priya, emerged from her room still in pajamas, phone clutched like a weapon. "Is this even legal?" she asked. "Can they just do that?"

Turns out, yes. In Oregon, landlords can raise rent with proper notice, and ours had given us the legally required ninety days. That didn't make it feel any less like a punch to the gut. We had thirty days to decide: pay up, or move out.

We sat down that night with a bottle of cheap wine that had been in our fridge since approximately the Bush administration and started doing the math. The old split was easy: $600 each. Now we were looking at $733 per person. That's $133 more every single month. Over a year, that's $1,596. Almost sixteen hundred dollars. For the exact same apartment with the exact same leaky faucet and the exact same window that doesn't close all the way in the living room.

Marcus was the first to crack. "I can't do it," he said, rubbing his temples. "I'm already eating ramen three nights a week. Where am I supposed to find an extra hundred thirty bucks?"

Priya was quieter. She worked part-time while finishing her master's, and I could see her doing mental calculations behind her eyes. "Maybe I could pick up another shift," she said, but her voice had that hollow quality that meant she already knew the answer was no.

I felt this weird mix of responsibility and helplessness. As the one with the "real" job — freelance UX design, which sounds more stable than it is — I technically could afford the increase. But that wasn't the point. The point was that we had built this little ecosystem together, this fragile balance of budgets and schedules and shared Netflix passwords, and now someone was coming in and knocking over the whole thing with a single email.

We decided to try negotiating. I drafted what I thought was a very professional, very reasonable email pointing out our history of on-time payments and asking if we could meet in the middle at a $200 increase instead. I hit send with the confidence of someone who had never actually negotiated with a landlord before.

He replied in four hours. One sentence: "The new rate is non-negotiable."

I wanted to scream. I actually did scream, into a pillow, while Biscuit watched from the doorway with the judgmental expression that only cats can pull off. Then I cried a little. Not proud of that part, but there it is.

So we started looking at apartments. And let me tell you, nothing makes you appreciate your current place like spending three weekends touring apartments that cost the same as your new rent but come with roommates you haven't met yet and bathrooms that smell like regret. We saw a place where the "second bedroom" was technically a walk-in closet. We saw another where the previous tenant had apparently been running some kind of unlicensed aquarium based on the water damage.

After the fourth disappointing tour, we sat in my car in a parking lot and just... stopped. Priya was eating drive-thru fries in the back seat. Marcus was scrolling through listings with the enthusiasm of someone reading a tax manual. And I was doing the math on moving costs.

Security deposit on a new place: $2,200. Moving truck: $150. Utility setup fees: $100. Time off work to move: $400 in lost billable hours. Plus the emotional cost of packing up three years of accumulated junk and figuring out who gets the blender. We were looking at close to $3,000 just to leave.

"What if we stayed?" I heard myself say.

They both looked at me like I'd suggested we start a cult.

"Hear me out," I said, pulling out my phone. "What if we don't split evenly anymore?"

The idea was simple and, in retrospect, kind of obvious. Priya had the smallest room — technically a den with a window that faced a brick wall. Marcus and I had the two real bedrooms. What if we adjusted the split based on room size instead of going equal thirds?

We ran the numbers. If Priya paid $550 for her tiny cave, Marcus paid $750 for the medium room, and I paid $900 for the master with the walk-in closet, it worked out to $2,200 total. Priya's increase was only $50 instead of $133. Marcus paid $150 more, which he could handle by cutting back on his expensive craft beer habit. And I paid $300 more, which stung, but I had the income to absorb it.

It wasn't perfect. It wasn't fair, exactly, in some cosmic sense. But it was a solution that kept us together and kept us in a place we actually liked.

We presented the new split to our landlord along with our signed lease renewal. He didn't care how we divided it up as long as the total hit his bank account. And just like that, the crisis that had felt like the end of the world became... manageable.

Looking back, what strikes me isn't the $400 increase. It's how close we came to blowing up a good living situation because we assumed the only options were "pay equally" or "move out." We never even considered a third path until we were desperate enough to think creatively.

These days, I keep a "rent shock" fund. Three hundred bucks a month goes into a separate savings account labeled "Landlord Surprises." If nothing happens, great — I have a vacation fund. If something does, I'm not scrambling.

And every time I walk past Priya's tiny brick-wall room, I smile. Because that little cave with no natural light? It saved our household. Sometimes the thing that looks like a problem is actually just a nudge toward a better arrangement.

Have you ever had a rent hike that forced you to rethink your whole setup? I'm curious what creative solutions other people have found. Because I have a feeling $400 is just the beginning for a lot of us.

— Jake Morrison, Freelance UX Designer, Portland, Oregon
